## YOUR JOB

You have been given the current PromptLaz application ZIP (named `PromptLaZ-main.zip`), the current PRD v1.5 markdown file inside it, and a separate master brief about building a global domain atlas and beginner-friendly prompt dropdowns.

Do not start by writing another generic proposal. **Inspect the actual repository, audit what already exists, research the missing areas, and return concrete assets that can be integrated into this project.**

PromptLaz is meant to be an Atlas of human knowledge and work for creating prompts. Users may know the outcome they want but not the discipline, subdiscipline, technical words, parameters, documents, or questions that matter. The interface should guide them through understandable choices and show the prompt assembling live.

A photographer may know they want a blurred portrait background without knowing which aperture to choose. A videographer may not know which frame rate, shutter angle, focal length, focus, codec or audio capture settings matter. A civil engineer may need concrete tests, soil mechanics, steel structures, structural analysis or fluid mechanics. Electrical engineers may need power systems, protection, electronics or telecom/RF. IT professionals may need software testing, networking, servers, data analysis, DevOps, cloud, cybersecurity or UI/UX. An accounting employee may need reconciliation, tax-document preparation or audit documentation. A factory worker may need paper/pulp process, process control or product quality. Users from other domains must receive the same treatment.

Experienced users must be able to select the known options and also enter custom details. Beginners must be able to use guided choices without having to invent technical terms first.

Your target is not a long list of categories. It is **a maintainable, source-backed knowledge system that powers useful choices, task-specific questions, and live prompt assembly**.

Do not claim that you can analyse literally the entire internet. Conduct systematic, wide-reaching research across credible web sources; document scope, evidence, gaps and limitations; keep the taxonomy extensible.

## PART 3 — RESEARCH THE KNOWLEDGE LANDSCAPE

Use web research and primary/authoritative sources. Research in layers: industry → occupation/role → discipline → subdiscipline → concept/tool/method/parameter → task → inputs → deliverable → quality checks.

Use multiple reference systems because no single source captures the whole problem:
- O*NET and O*NET-SOC as one US-based source for occupations, skills, work activities, technology skills and task statements.
- ESCO as a multilingual European source of occupation, skill and knowledge concepts, preferred/non-preferred terms and relationships.
- UN ISIC and relevant national industry classifications to understand economic sectors and industry sub-branches.
- UNESCO/OECD fields-of-research classifications for academic and scientific disciplines.
- Professional engineering/scientific bodies, university curricula, regulator websites, official standards catalogues, vendor documentation, technical handbooks, job descriptions, task manuals and reputable practitioner sources for specific technical detail.
- Additional regional sources for global breadth. Include India-specific occupational/industry terminology where useful, but do not treat India or any single jurisdiction as the whole world.
- Credible practitioner communities may help discover common real-world wording and pain points; do not treat anecdotal community content as an authority for standards or safety.

For every research source, record title, publisher, URL, date accessed, source type, region/jurisdiction, what finding it supports, confidence and limits. Do not fabricate citations and do not claim a page supports more than it does. Respect licenses/copyright; summarize concepts rather than copying protected source material.

## PART 5 — MAKE THE CHOICES TEACH, WITHOUT OVERLOADING THE USER

The user experience must preserve:
- searchable dropdowns;
- a progressive hierarchy that filters children based on parent choices;
- an explicit “Other / enter my own” choice at each relevant level;
- known parent selections combined with user-entered custom child/task/details;
- “I'm not sure — help me choose” paths for novices;
- direct search for experts;
- live prompt preview;
- local draft save/restore and copy/export;
- clear, truthful taxonomy/workflow maturity badges.

For each selectable option, write concise novice-facing content:
- `label`
- `shortDescription`
- `chooseWhen`
- `example`
- `aliases`
- `technicalTerms` (optional)
- `relatedNodeIds`
- `relatedWorkflowIds`
- `coverageStatus`
- `sourceIds`
- `reviewStatus`

For tasks with specialist parameters, add context-specific question-bank blueprints. Do not attach every parameter to every task. Use progressive disclosure:
- beginner path: goal, situation, known information, desired output;
- practitioner path: relevant domain parameters, methods, constraints and data;
- expert path: advanced settings and standards that matter to the chosen task.

Use a question `importance` such as `required`, `optional`, `expert`; include `dependsOn`/conditional rules where appropriate. Every question should explain what it means and why the answer matters. Unknown answers must be handled explicitly. Never invent consequential measurements or standards.

When a user chooses “Other”, retain selected parents and custom child/task text simultaneously. Keep an unclassified custom term usable without marking it as reviewed. Allow users to suggest a missing topic for future editorial review, but do not silently publish user-entered terms into the authoritative taxonomy.
